02

The pattern you watch walk through the door

Named in writing, by a consultant psychiatrist, about me.

Addiction is often rooted in trauma. But I think something is being missed, and with your help I think I can prove it. For me, and for a group of adults you will recognise, untreated ADHD is driving, recycling and fanning the fire of the addiction, regardless of origin.

For twenty-five years, self-medication worked for me — even though I had no idea I was treating my own ADHD. Until it didn’t. Alcohol or substances can do what an untreated ADHD brain has been trying to do all along: supply motivation, sustain attention, create focus and cut the noise. It feels like function, not fun. That is not a moral failure. And then it crosses the line into dependence, floods a reward system that turns down its own sensitivity and erodes the very systems recovery depends on.

In April 2026 an independent consultant psychiatrist wrote to the CPFT Adult ADHD Team about my care and named the pattern in one sentence:

“Adult ADHD previously complicated with excess alcohol use to self-medicate inadequately managed ADHD symptoms.”

Read the order of it. Not ADHD plus alcohol, treated separately. ADHD — complicated by self-medication. That letter is about me. It proves that what happened to me has a name, in writing.

Recovery is ninety per cent mindset. But to get to that ninety per cent, the mind has to be at a hundred per cent working order in the first place — and with ADHD it isn’t. I am no doctor. I am someone it happened to, but I have sat in enough rooms, and helped enough others start their own ADHD journey, to believe I am not the only one.

One man’s experience is not evidence about a population, and I am not going to pretend it is. But I have seen it for years — in fellowships, in rehabs, in detoxes — and I see it more clearly now I am medicated than I ever did while I was in it. That is why I think the question is worth asking. It is not why I think I already know the answer.

03

How the system met me

It saw what was on fire. It never asked what was burning.

As you know, I reached CGL through a courtroom. Police and probation met the addiction long before anyone asked what was driving it. I was funded for residential rehab by CGL — after a year of turning up, and a panel that said yes — at a dual-diagnosis hospital staffed by remarkable people doing real care. But on arrival my ADHD medication was removed, because it is a stimulant. I had chosen it for exactly that reason. I declared the prescription in my admission paperwork, weeks before I arrived. They confiscated it at the door anyway — a prescribed medication that was, I should point out, only mildly effective, which is exactly why I have been sitting on the medication review list: to move from methylphenidate to lisdexamfetamine, the change a private reassessment has since confirmed and the NHS review would surface.

I came out fully twelve-stepped and addiction-informed, but with nothing underneath holding the brain that started all of it. No medication and no self-medication: over that first year I was experiencing my full ADHD for the first time, but I only realised that after self-medication proved, one final time, that it is not for me. The recovery work was not wrong. My brain could not use it properly without the ADHD driver being treated.

I lasted two years. The relapse wasn’t random — it was the same brain seeking what it was still missing. Then, as you know, I engaged with my whole support network, including you, forced a final detox and was correctly medicated. My life changed within days.

I had been diagnosed privately, and for a while I was on shared care with my GP. It was lost at a handoff when I changed surgery. I was referred to the ADHD team in March 2021. Five years on, I am still waiting for the medication review. In that time I cycled through A&E, addiction services and mental health teams, and no part of the system connected the loop.

Not because the people weren’t good. They were. But the rehab’s fierce twelve-step model advertised ADHD as a dual diagnosis — it still does to this day, and it is how and why I found the rehab. Yet they didn’t treat a brain that increasingly suspected it was being mis-described — and the clinical letter, years later, said so in writing.

And the problem is not history. Diagnosed in 2020, referred in 2021, once on shared care — and to this day the letters A-D-H-D do not appear anywhere on MyChart, in my NHS Login, or even on my GP record. I have had to find the ADHD Team myself and ask for their help to get shared care — and they have referred my request on to the Central East ICB. Other surgeries will only take on shared care from a psychiatrist on the Right to Choose list, and sadly mine was not. That is another bump in the road for this cohort, but that part is the ADHD Team’s, not yours. The system still cannot see the driver in me. That is why this cohort needs identifying, with urgency. They will be both known and unknown, but discoverable and targetable — and most are highly likely to be visible already within CGL, CRS and the ADHD Team.

04

Why good work does not hold

The order of operations, and it is not optional.

Services respond to the addiction, and rightly so. But if the ADHD stays untouched, the work does not hold. Therapy, recovery groups and relapse prevention work land on a brain that cannot sequence or retain them. People are being asked to recover using the exact executive functions their unmanaged ADHD has impaired.

I have lined up what successful addiction recovery requires against what unmanaged ADHD impairs — item for item. It makes my point very clear.

  • Executive function — ADHD can impair planning, sequencing and self-regulation; recovery requires daily planning, structure and delayed gratification.
  • Focus & attention — ADHD can impair focus on anything non-stimulating; recovery requires sustained attention through long therapy work.
  • Working memory — ADHD can impair holding strategies, appointments, the thread; recovery requires retaining tools and tracking triggers.
  • Impulse control — ADHD can impair the pause before acting; recovery requires resisting cravings in the moment.
  • Emotional regulation — ADHD can impair tolerating distress that arrives fast; recovery requires riding out craving without the substance.
  • Routine & follow-through — ADHD can impair sustaining unrewarding routines; recovery requires repetition that rebuilds new patterns.
  • Reward & arousal — ADHD can impair waiting for a delayed reward; recovery requires a stable baseline.

Read it as a model, not a verdict — it is not a research finding and people vary. Whether it holds in a real local caseload is exactly what that first report would establish.

The medication does not do the recovery work. It creates the conditions where it can finally land. I know that from the inside: the step work, the groups, therapy, the relapse prevention I did with you. All of it was excellent, and I thought I had cracked it — but now I understand my brain could not retain or recall it after the initial scrubbing it had.

Once the ADHD was treated, everything else I’d worked on finally started to hold.

And it is not only cost. Once my ADHD was treated, my own work rate did not just recover — it went up. Every cost model counts what a treated adult stops costing; none of them counts what he puts back. The avoided-cost side is the conservative side of the ledger.

Inertiia — SMART sits exactly here. It is a CBT-based recovery programme, and everything it asks of a person — notice the trigger, remember the tool, pause before acting, do it again tomorrow — runs on the same machinery. NICE already backs the combination: for adults who have benefited from medication but are still impaired, it recommends considering non-pharmacological treatment alongside it (NG87, rec 1.5.17). The papers I have read point the same way: a treated brain can use the programme. I would find that a very interesting conversation to have with you.

So, for me at least, there is a clear order. One — the diagnosis and medical intervention. The brain gets what it needs to function. Two — then the re-wiring. The routines, behavioural reinforcement, coping mechanisms and everything your service already does well, now landing on equipment that can hold it. Three — then repeat again, and again. Re-wiring is repetition, and repetition runs on days, not appointments.

I know what the guidance says. Stabilise the substance misuse first; in uncontrolled and chaotic use, stimulants are not routinely recommended (CR235). The same guidance says that “when prescribed stimulants are used appropriately, there is little evidence of patients becoming dependent, and indeed treating ADHD may reduce the lifetime risk of developing a substance use disorder” (CR235 §11.1). I was stabilised first, more than once, and each time the driver went untreated. The sequence belongs to the clinicians. What is required is cohort identification, upstream intervention, and increased connection and continuity.

05

The gaps — and why nobody owns them

Every service owns its episode. Nobody is accountable for the time in between.

The gaps are not where support stops. They are where the entire mechanism of recovery is supposed to happen — unsupervised, by the person least equipped to run it.

Nobody owns the risk in the gap. And the gap is not one gap. It starts the moment somebody first suspects ADHD, and it runs through the referral, the wait, the assessment, the medication review, and every handoff from one service into the next, while the ADHD is allowed to pour itself, like an accelerant, onto the addiction. At every one of those joins a person can go quiet with nobody downstream knowing. CGL — or CRS, holding the aftercare — should be alerted when one of its people is waiting on an ADHD update but relapsing constantly, spiralling in and out of A&E while they wait — which is simply what happened, or rather didn’t happen, to me. I appreciate ADHD does not sit under mental health; and even if it did, sobriety is required before treatment — which is exactly the point. Every broken thread in this story is an upstream intervention waiting to happen.

I know how busy you are, and that key workers cannot check in every day, or even every week — which I think everyone would agree simply isn’t enough connection for addiction and recovery work to hold, regardless of ADHD.

The question is not how often a service can call. It is what happens in all the time in between — because that is where the repetition has to live, and the person living it is the one whose condition makes repetition hardest.

This is where CRS lives. Your own front door says it — connection matters, recovery is possible — and you mean it: Recovery Cafés every week in six towns, open to anyone at any stage, and to the people who love them. Aftercare is the gap, by design: the person has left the structured part and is trying to re-integrate — community, work, family, a life that holds. That is personal growth through connection, and it is the part I found hardest — the first time, in the year after rehab, when I do not think I even reached CRS. It runs on exactly the executive functions the untreated ADHD had taken, and a brain that cannot hold the thread cannot grow along it. The second time, medicated, I was through CGL in days and CRS in weeks, signed off as doing well — because I was. Same me, same services. The difference was what the brain could hold. Connection matters. Recovery is possible. Continuity is what keeps them both in reach.

Not a service. Not a caseworker. Nothing clinical. Continuity fills the gaps. Think of it as neuroplasticity for the system. Just as a treated ADHD brain can finally form new patterns, a connected set of services learns too — every receipt and every reply teaches the route to hold the next person a little better than the last.

The Continuity Infrastructure

A non-clinical layer that holds the thread between services.

Every service owns its own episode. Nobody owns the time between them. That is not a criticism of anyone — it is how the system is built. But it means the most dangerous period in this journey is the one period that belongs to nobody. It is not a gap in care. It is unowned risk.

OTOS asks no organisation to take on that risk, that liability or that caseload. It fills the space intelligently with continuous connection, pre-agreed service updates, and behavioural reinforcement with addiction education — so the gap stops belonging to nobody and becomes genuinely useful. The person is no longer stuck on their own. Not joining up the services — holding the person across them, and updating them with agreed, Continuity-only updates.

The clinical notes stay where they are. The continuity signal travels. Every drift signal requires a human review decision before any action. No autonomous clinical output.

It is consent-led: nobody is on it without agreeing to be. The person opts in, agrees the services can share access to their thread, and the thread only ever shows specific, pre-agreed information.

What OTOS Continuity is, and what it is not

✕ What OTOS is not

  • Not a clinical service. It does not diagnose, prescribe or treat.
  • Not an EPR or clinical record. No record access, no write-back.
  • Not a connection between services’ systems. No integration and no feeds.
  • Not a route for notes or case files to move between services.
  • Not a replacement for any existing service.
  • Not crisis care. It makes no safeguarding or clinical decisions.
  • Not surveillance. No device monitoring, no tracking.
  • Not a finished product. It is a bounded demonstrator, and any savings are modelled potential, not promised.

✓ What OTOS is

  • A non-clinical continuity layer around services that already exist.
  • Not joining up the services — holding the person across them.
  • Focused on one defined, costly cohort, carrying only signals agreed in advance.
  • The clinical notes stay where they are. The continuity signal travels.
  • Consent-led and tokenised. No NHS number required by default.
  • Handoff receipts — so you can see whether the next step actually happened.
  • Drift visibility turns silence into something a keyworker can review.
  • Authorised view. Separate record. Same thread. Partners see each other’s agreed status posts, never each other’s notes or systems.

OTOS surfaces the signals. The partner team makes every decision. Nothing changes in how you work. You just see more of what already happened.

For the services, in practice: waiting-list silence becomes a signal instead of a blind spot, micro check-ins keep contact going between keyworker calls, and a person reviews every signal before anything is done.

What I designed to be in the gaps

I should be clear about what this is. OTOS Continuity is infrastructure. It does not diagnose, prescribe, treat or replace anyone. OTOS surfaces the signals. The partner team makes every decision. Nothing changes in how your existing service works. I am not going to pretend it is nothing at all — it is a login, and a few minutes to enrol someone and set your own signals. That is the trade. What you get back is a whole-journey view of the person, kept up to date — visibility, and more connection and continuity across every service involved.

One user thread per person, with every event logged on that thread and visible to every authorised partner. It is consent-led: nobody is on it without agreeing to be. Services do not connect to each other, and nobody’s clinical record is touched — each partner connects to Continuity, and it is a shared view of one journey, not a pipe between organisations. Every interaction is also a signal.

What you would actually do with it — spelled out

You are not being asked to run anything. I have designed a system, and it does five plain things for a service like yours:

  • You add a person to it. Someone on your caseload agrees to be on it; you enrol them from your own login, once. That is the whole onboarding. And when you suspect ADHD is driving the pattern, CRS can raise it through Continuity exactly as CGL can — same login, same route.
  • The person is held between appointments and your calls. Small, agreed prompts we call “Micro-Checkins” go out automatically in the gaps — drawn from your own programme’s language, at a frequency the person agrees with their key worker. Every reply, and even an unanswered delivery, lands on that person’s thread as a signal — not in your inbox. A fortnightly call becomes a fortnightly call plus a thread that never goes quiet.
  • You see what the other services around them have logged — a referral made, an A&E presentation, an appointment attended, a Wellbeing Hub class offered, a discharge, an ADHD step reached — on that one person’s thread, to the extent each service has agreed to share. Never another service’s clinical notes. Never their record.
  • You set your own signals. What counts as drift for your service — a missed reply, a missed session, three quiet days — is yours to define. When it trips, your named reviewer gets the alert.
  • You keep a receipted thread for every person on it: what was sent, what was answered, what was handed on and whether it landed. Something you can point to in a review, or in front of a commissioner.

OTOS carries the technical layer; consent, governance and operating rules are agreed with participating partners. OTOS Continuity is purely infrastructure, simply strengthening connection and communication for a cohort that I believe is crying out for it. The people, the words and the decisions stay yours.

  • Every morning is a data point. A reply says someone is still there. Even a delivery receipt — the phone is on — is information you did not have yesterday.

None of that is new work for a key worker. Same caseload, with the silences filled in.

What it does not do

  • Does not replace or write into the clinical record — it holds only the minimum agreed continuity data and receipts required for the test
  • Does not add paperwork — one login, nothing to install, hand-held onboarding; and my idea is that the Micro-Checkins and lifestyle “waiting” messages are co-written with the RCE Wellbeing Hub
  • Does not make clinical decisions — every signal goes to a named human reviewer in your team
  • Does not require an NHS number — tokenised IDs by default. Not surveillance
  • Does not prioritise or fast-track anyone independently — although effective early intervention would need fast-track discussions between the relevant parties
We plan to measure partner burden and treat it as a pre-pilot success criterion. If it takes too long, we will adjust accordingly.

The evidence, and one example

ADHD in addiction is not a private hunch.

NICE’s own guideline tells clinicians to expect more ADHD among people with a history of substance misuse (NG87, rec 1.2.1), and names self-medication as a risk to discuss at diagnosis (rec 1.4.3). The Royal College of Psychiatrists says significant comorbid substance misuse should be managed jointly between adult and addiction services (CR235) — and, in the same guidance, says stabilise the substance misuse first and be cautious with stimulants in chaotic use. Both constraints are real, and they belong in the design, not the small print.

Your own organisation has published work on assessing ADHD inside substance-use services — the Nottinghamshire assessment pathway for adults using substances within the criminal justice system (Hammond, Murphy & Huke, Clinical Psychology Forum, November 2024; practice development, not a trial). CGL Nottingham is not OTOS proof. It is pathway proof. This is not me asking you to leap into the unknown. The recognition already exists inside your own world.

It was being found — and then somebody staying with it. The study shows the first half. The second half is what I want to test here.

Those numbers are hard to ignore, and once you have seen them you cannot unsee them. I should acknowledge there was no control group, but it is what happened in one study in France and intentionally similar in shape to the “intervention plus continuity” concept I am trying to build: ADHD found, treated and held, then the recovery work has the chance to deliver higher success rates. You already have a view on how many of your people that is, and your view is worth more than anything I could cite.

The plan — three stages

Each one earned by the one before it. Each one someone else’s to make.

  • A research report — one question. This one is mine to carry: I am proposing it to Cambridgeshire Public Health as a first, small piece of commissioned analysis, to support what I am asking you for here. The question, exactly as it would be commissioned: “Is ADHD materially under-recognised among people at the intersection of addiction and mental-health complexity in Cambridgeshire — and what would the system need to know before deciding whether to act?” What it finds is the report’s to say, not mine to assume — and it has to be able to conclude that there is no signal. I have spent nine months trying to turn something I survived into a question that can be disproved. Just yesterday I wrote to Professor Samuele Cortese at the University of Southampton, asking whether the question stands up scientifically — including whether it doesn’t — and whether he would consider acting as Principal Investigator on a funded research route to getting this recognised and proven, alongside the technology I have built.
  • A bounded pre-pilot. If the answer is yes: small, time-limited, no new services onboarded. Participants pre-selected and consenting, the thread running for a fixed window, and everything measured — including your time.
  • A pilot. Only if the pre-pilot holds. Someone else’s decision, made on what the first two stages actually showed.

Nobody is being asked to commit to stage two by looking at stage one. And the medical intervention is the point of all of it — getting people to assessment and treatment sooner, and holding them while they wait.
